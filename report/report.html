Optical spike router: what we did and what we found

Working notes for understanding, not a paper. Data from 19–20 October 2026 (lab PC runs in runs/, simulation in sim/).

One-paragraph summary. We used the photonic chip's MZI mesh as a small optical switchboard that carries the busiest spike traffic between five emulated neuromorphic cores. After calibrating the lasers, detectors and heaters, and fixing a detector readout problem and a laser cross-talk problem, the router delivered 39,812 replayed spikes with 29 errors (7.3×10−4), 27 of them during software stalls (2 in the 38,810 time-kept slots), switched routes in about 35 µs, carried 58% of all spike packets, and left the network's accuracy unchanged (91.0%). A model of the shared bus shows this removes queueing delay when traffic approaches the bus's limit; a scaling study shows the benefit holds best when traffic is concentrated on a few core pairs.

1. The goal

A spiking neural network (SNN) on a neuromorphic chip is split across several cores. Every spike that leaves a core is sent as a packet over one shared electronic bus. Spiking activity comes in bursts, so the bus periodically gets crowded and packets wait.

Idea: add a few optical express lanes. Each core gets a laser (to send) and a photodiode (to receive); the chip's MZI mesh acts as a switchboard connecting the busiest sender→receiver pairs with light. Everything else stays on the bus. A controller re-points the switchboard as traffic changes. One rule: each photodiode listens to only one sender at a time (otherwise it couldn't tell who fired); one sender may feed several receivers by splitting its light (multicast).

2. The setup

  • Chip: 6 waveguide rows, 13 MZIs arranged in 5 columns. Each MZI has a heater ("r_c" = MZI joining rows r and r+1 in column c).
  • Lasers: six, one per row, driven by a USB-3114 analog output (0–10 V).
  • Heaters: driven by an AD5370 DAC through a Pico microcontroller over USB serial.
  • Detectors: 16 on-chip photodiodes read by an NI USB-6210: one at each row's input (PD_r_0), one at each row's output (PD_r_5), and four inside the mesh.
  • Workflow: scripts written on the Mac, run on the lab PC, results pushed back through git and analysed on the Mac.

3. What we did, step by step

Step 1: getting the software and a first look

  • Fixed the Python project file (it pointed to Mac-only library paths), freed disk space, and installed the libraries.
  • Wrote check_setup.py: checks the boards connect, measures detector noise (about 0.25 nA) and which laser lights which row.
  • Each laser feeds its own row (laser k → row k).
  • First surprise: the light was very weak. At the 7 V we first used, each laser produced only ~15 nA. At 10 V it was ~100× more. Reason below.

Step 2: understanding the mesh

With all heaters at 0 V, each laser's light came out at a very specific output. That pattern matches a simple rule: at 0 V every MZI sits close to CROSS, so light zig-zags one row per column. This also confirmed the mesh layout we had inferred from the wiring names. The four internal detectors lit up exactly where the zig-zag passes.

Step 3: calibrating the lasers (laser_calibration.py)

  • Stepped each laser from 0 to 10 V. Lasing starts between 7.1 and 7.5 V (the threshold); below that the laser only glows weakly. That explains the dim light in Step 1.
  • On/off contrast between 0 V and 10 V: 37–40 dB (about 5,000×).
  • Pulse test: pulses keep the same height whether isolated or repeated (no heating effect at our speeds).

Step 4: calibrating the heaters (heater_sweep.py)

For each MZI, we lit one laser whose light passes through it, swept the heater from 0 to 4.2 V, and watched the two outputs trade light. A fitted curve gives the voltage for CROSS, 50:50 and BAR.

  • 12 of 13 MZIs work cleanly (near-perfect fits). BAR needs 3.1–4.2 V; CROSS is at or near 0 V.
  • MZI 1_5 is faulty: in 2 of 4 runs it jumped suddenly near 1 V and then stopped responding. We never drive it.
  • Some MZIs cannot reach a perfect CROSS below 4.2 V. Your senior colleague's note (heaters stop tuning near 5 V) confirmed we should not go higher. Heaters are ~200 Ω cold, up to ~400 Ω hot.

Step 5: choosing the router: 5×5 on rows 2–6

  • A fully programmable 6×6 needs 15 MZIs in 6 columns; the chip has 13 in 5. So the full 6×6 cannot make every routing pattern.
  • Any five neighbouring rows form a complete 5×5. We use rows 2–6 because rows 1–5 would depend on the faulty 1_5.
  • Row 1 is closed off by holding 1_1 and 1_3 at BAR. 1_5 stays at 0 V, where it sits near CROSS, so the light for core C1 simply exits on PD_1_5.

Step 6: mapping the neural network onto the chip

  • Network: 2,312 inputs (event-camera pixels) → 128 → 64 → 10 outputs, trained on N-MNIST digits. Accuracy 90.9%.
  • Five cores: C1 = top half of the image, C2 = bottom half, C3/C4 = the two halves of the first hidden layer, C5 = second hidden layer + outputs.
  • Where traffic goes: C1 and C2 both send to C3 and C4; C3 and C4 both send to C5. So each receiver has two competing senders.
  • Two heaters do all the switching: 2_2 chooses which input core (C1 or C2) multicasts to C3+C4 (split 50:50); 4_2 chooses which hidden core (C3 or C4) sends to C5. The four combinations are settings A–D.
  • No matrix optimisation is needed. Every MZI is only BAR, CROSS or 50:50, and light from different lasers does not interfere, so a route is just a table of heater voltages.

4. Problems we found, and how we fixed them

Problem 1: the detector readout leaked between channels

The NI board measures its 16 detectors one after another through one shared input. If it moves on too fast, the next measurement still contains part of the previous one. Each detector "inherited" about a third of the detector read just before it. This created fake light in places light cannot reach, distorted some calibrations, and made every reading about one third too low.

Fix: slow the reading so each detector has 100 µs to settle (read_pds() in check_setup.py). Our first idea (read each channel twice) was rejected by the NI driver. After the fix the leak measured about 0%, and we re-ran the calibrations.

Problem 2: lasers changed each other's power

To make all lasers equally bright, we first ran them at slightly different voltages (9.1–9.9 V). Then switching one laser on changed another's power by up to 37%, instantly (so electrical, not heat). At 10 V the effect disappeared (below 2%). All lasers now run at 10 V. Their brightness differs a little, which is harmless because each receiver gets its own detection threshold. We don't yet know exactly why 10 V is immune (the lasers are not saturating there); it does not matter for the results.

Problem 3: the PC sometimes pauses the program

Windows occasionally stalls Python for up to ~90 ms, so a laser isn't pulsed on time. We now flag any time slot where a command ran more than 3 ms late as a "timing fault" and count its errors separately, so software hiccups are not blamed on the optics. The script also runs at high priority.

5. Measurements of the router

Routing accuracy and crosstalk (route_check.py)

For each setting: set all heaters, turn on each laser alone, and measure where its light goes; then turn on the two active lasers together.

  • 95–99% of each sender's light reaches its intended receivers.
  • The multicast splits evenly (within 0.4 dB).
  • Crosstalk 15–28 dB below the signal at 11 of 12 receivers (one at 14.4 dB). Light from two lasers adds up within 2%.

Switching speed (switch_timing.py)

  • Recorded one detector 250,000 times per second while flipping 2_2 or 4_2, 20 times each way.
  • A route switches in about 32–36 µs (10–90%) and is fully settled in about 50 µs. Our detectors themselves take about 20 µs (checked by switching a laser), so part of the measured time is the detector.
  • The light starts moving about 1 ms after Python sends the command: that is our USB/Pico path, not the chip.

Continuous recording (stream_check.py)

The board can record detectors continuously while lasers pulse: all 400 pulse detections (80 pulses × 5 detectors) were correct, including 5 ms pulses, with zero false pulses. Laser switching causes brief one-sample electrical glitches on other detectors; we ignore them by requiring two consecutive samples above threshold.

Checking the row-6 detector (pd_check.py)

A colleague mentioned there might be no detectors on row 6. PD_6_5 passed three tests: it follows laser 5 on/off (1,528 nA vs 3 nA, five cycles); the mid-chip row-6 detector PD_6_3 lights with it; and switching to setting B moves the light away to PD_3_5 (PD_6_5 drops to 0.6%). So row 6 has a working output detector.

6. Sending the network's real spikes (spike_replay.py)

  • The network runs in software; its spike pattern is played through the chip in slow motion. Each 2 ms network step becomes a 15 ms slot with a 6 ms laser pulse for every sender that fired.
  • Slow because every laser and heater command goes through Python and USB (about 1–3 ms each). The optics are roughly 1,000× faster.
  • A pulse means "this core fired during this step"; which neurons fired is not sent by light (that would need much faster modulation).
  • Route changes happen in the quiet gap between pulses, so they never cut a spike.

7. Does it relieve congestion?

The bus is a software model (there is no physical bus to congest), so this part is modelled, but it uses the measured delivery from the replay. Carrying 58% of packets by light is like making the bus 2.4× faster. Spike traffic is bursty (peaks 4–5× the average), so a bus sized for the average still queues at the peaks; the lanes remove most of that queueing.

Honest catch. This small network sends only ~35 packets per millisecond, far below what real neuromorphic buses handle. A real chip running it would not congest. The result applies when a chip's traffic approaches its bus limit (bigger networks, more neurons per core, or slow low-power buses).

8. Does it scale? (sim/scaling.py, simulation)

We split the network over more cores (up to 33) and ran the same controller on an ideal larger mesh, for two networks: the original fully connected one, and a variant where each first-layer neuron only sees one patch of the image (locally connected, 88.2% accuracy).

  • Why it drops: each receiver can listen to only one sender at a time, and in a fully connected network every core talks to every other.
  • Locality keeps it useful: when traffic is concentrated on a few core pairs, about half still goes by light at 33 cores.
  • Reconfiguring matters more with more cores (dynamic beats a fixed setting by up to 35%).
  • Optical limits (estimated from our measured margins): light power allows ~30-port meshes; crosstalk limits it to about 20 ports with this chip's MZI quality.

10. What it means

Optical express lanes can carry the busiest spike traffic between cores with essentially no errors and no loss of accuracy, switch routes in ~35 µs, and relieve bus congestion. They work best when traffic is concentrated on a few core pairs, the same reason hybrid electrical/optical switching works in data centres (a slow optical switch for the few heavy flows, electronics for the rest).

Limitations to keep in mind

  • Only 5 cores; the cores and the bus are emulated in software. Only the optical path is real.
  • Slow motion: 7.5× slower than real time, because lasers and heaters are commanded through Python and USB.
  • A light pulse carries "this core fired", not which neurons fired.
  • Lasers at 10 V (unequal brightness); MZI 1_5 unreliable; one receiver at 14.4 dB crosstalk.
  • Power: ~0.4 W of heater power just to hold a setting, so energy is not a selling point. Lower-power phase shifters exist.
  • Beyond 5 cores, results are modelled (ideal mesh assumed; crosstalk growth estimated).
